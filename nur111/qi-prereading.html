Why quality improvement

Supports LO1LO7

In 1999 the Institute of Medicine’s report To Err Is Human estimated that 44,000 to 98,000 people died each year in U.S. hospitals because of medical error. Its main argument still shapes nursing today: most errors come from flawed systems, not bad people.

Key concept

Quality improvement (QI) is the ongoing work of measuring care, finding where it falls short, and changing the system so it does better. QI asks one question: Is our system keeping clients safe?

The language of safety events

Supports LO1LO2

Two questions sort almost every event. Did it reach the client? If it did, how much harm did it cause?

EventReached the client?HarmExample
Near miss (close call)NoNoneBarcode scan flags the wrong medication before it is given
No-harm eventYesNoneA scheduled dose is given 2 hours early; the client’s condition does not change
Adverse eventYesHarmMissed repositioning leads to a stage 2 pressure injury
Sentinel eventYesDeath, permanent harm, or severe harmA fall that fractures a hip and requires surgery

Key concept

The Joint Commission defines a sentinel event as a patient safety event, not primarily related to the client’s illness, that reaches the client and results in death, permanent harm, or severe harm. Every sentinel event calls for an immediate investigation into its root causes.

Safety flag

Some events count as sentinel because of what they are: surgery on the wrong site, wrong client, or wrong procedure; a foreign object left in after a procedure; a transfusion of incompatible blood; a suicide in a 24-hour staffed setting; an infant abduction.

“Sentinel event” and “never event” are not the same term. Never events are a fixed list from the National Quality Forum. The two lists overlap a lot, but they come from different organizations.

What the data show

Hospitals reported 1,575 sentinel events to The Joint Commission in 2024. Falls are the most common type. Reporting to The Joint Commission is voluntary, so a rise in reports may mean more honest reporting, not necessarily more harm.

When The Joint Commission looks for causes, the same contributing factors come up again and again: breakdowns in communication, breakdowns in teamwork, and policies not followed. Others include look-alike or sound-alike medications, similar client names, interruptions, fatigue, and short staffing.

Reporting: what the nurse does next

Supports LO3LO4

After any safety event, the order of your actions matters:

  1. Make the client safe. Assess and intervene first.
  2. Notify the charge nurse and the provider.
  3. Document the objective facts and the care you gave in the health record.
  4. Complete an occurrence report (also called an incident or safety report) in the facility’s reporting system.

Clinical pearl

The occurrence report is a quality document, not part of the client’s chart. Do not write “incident report filed” in the health record. And report near misses too: a near miss shows the same weak spot in the system as a serious event, at no cost to the client.

Reporting is a professional, ethical, and legal duty. The ANA Code of Ethics calls on nurses to protect the health, safety, and rights of clients, facility policy requires reporting, and the state board of nursing expects it. One report is a single data point. When risk management sees fifty reports about the same look-alike vials, the storage gets fixed.

Just culture

Supports LO6

Blaming the nurse who made the last mistake leaves the flawed system in place for the next nurse. A just culture responds to the behavior behind an error, not to how bad the outcome was.

BehaviorWhat it looks likeResponse
Human errorAn unintended slip or lapseConsole the nurse and fix the system
At-risk behaviorA shortcut whose risk isn’t recognized or seems justifiedCoach, and remove the reason for the shortcut
Reckless behaviorKnowingly disregarding a substantial, unjustifiable riskSanction: discipline is appropriate

A just culture is not blame-free. It holds the system and the individual accountable for their own parts. It also makes people more willing to report, because they know an honest mistake will lead to a fix and not a punishment.

The QI cycle: Plan–Do–Study–Act

Supports LO3LO7

Units improve by testing small changes and measuring the result, over and over. The Institute for Healthcare Improvement’s PDSA cycle is the most common model. Here is how a unit might use it on its fall rate.

StepWhat happensExample: falls on a medical unit
PlanName the problem, predict what a change will do, and choose how to measure itMost falls happen at night on the way to the bathroom. Predict that scheduled toileting rounds will reduce them. Measure falls per 1,000 patient days.
DoTry the change on a small scale and collect dataNight shift on one unit tries the rounds for 6 weeks.
StudyCompare the results with the baseline and the predictionDid falls drop? Did anything else change, such as staffing or reporting?
ActAdopt, adapt, or abandon the change, then start the next cycleKeep the rounds, adjust the timing, or try something else.

Key concept

A benchmark is a reference point for judging results. A unit can compare itself with its own past performance (internal) or with similar units nationally (external). Rates are usually given per 1,000 patient days, so units of different sizes can be compared fairly.

Many QI measures are nurse-sensitive, meaning nursing care directly affects them: falls with injury, pressure injuries, catheter-associated urinary tract infections (CAUTI), and central line–associated bloodstream infections (CLABSI).

Case: two names, one hallway

Supports LO2LO4LO5LO6

3 East, 2100. Mr. Hollis (room 312) and Mr. Hollins (room 314) are both receiving scheduled basal insulin at bedtime. Their insulin pens are stored in the same drawer of the medication room. The nurse has been interrupted three times in the last 15 minutes.

ClientMr. Hollis, 312
Fingerstick glucose168 mg/dL
OrderedBasal insulin, 2100
Bedside scanWrong-patient alert

The nurse takes a pen from the drawer, goes to room 312, and scans Mr. Hollis’s wristband and the pen. The scanner shows that the pen belongs to Mr. Hollins. Nothing has been given.

Safety flag

Insulin is a high-alert medication: an error can cause serious harm quickly. An insulin pen is for one client only and is never shared, even with a new needle.

1. What kind of event is this, and what should the nurse do now?

A near miss: the error was caught before it reached the client. The nurse gets Mr. Hollis’s own pen, verifies it with two identifiers and a scan, gives the dose, and then files a report. The near miss still gets reported, because the next nurse may not have a working scanner.

2. What contributing factors set this up?

Similar names on the same hall, pens for both clients stored in one drawer, and repeated interruptions during medication preparation. Not one of these is about a “bad nurse.” All of them are about the system.

3. How would a just culture respond to the nurse? Name a change the unit could test with PDSA.

This was human error, an unintended slip, so the response is to console the nurse and fix the system. Changes to test include name-alert stickers, a separate labeled bin for each client’s pens, a no-interruption zone for medication preparation, or moving one client to another room. In Study, the unit would compare wrong-patient scanner alerts before and after the change.

Check yourself

Supports LO1LO2LO3LO6

These are for practice only and are not graded. Commit to an answer before you open the explanation.

Supports LO1LO2

1. A client receives a unit of blood that is not compatible with their blood type, develops a hemolytic reaction, and is moved to the ICU. Which term fits best?

  1. Near miss
  2. No-harm event
  3. Adverse event
  4. Sentinel event
Answer and rationale

D. Sentinel event. An ABO-incompatible transfusion is on The Joint Commission’s sentinel event list, and this one caused severe harm.

A: A near miss never reaches the client. This one did.

B: There was harm, so it is not a no-harm event.

C: It is an adverse event, but “sentinel” is more specific because of the harm level and the event type.

Supports LO6

2. A nurse moving between two call lights programs an IV pump at 125 mL/hr when 12.5 mL/hr was ordered. The nurse catches it 10 minutes later, and the client is unharmed. In a just culture, which response fits best?

  1. Suspend the nurse
  2. Console the nurse and look at the system
  3. Take no action because there was no harm
  4. Require the nurse to retake orientation
Answer and rationale

B. This is an unintended slip, which is human error. The response is support for the nurse plus a look at the system: interruptions, workload, and whether the pump has dose-limit software.

A: Punishing a slip makes people stop reporting and leaves the system unchanged.

C: No harm does not mean no action. This is a no-harm event and should still be reported, because the same conditions could cause harm next time.

D: Retraining fixes a knowledge gap. This nurse knew the correct rate, so retraining doesn’t address the cause.

Supports LO3

3. A unit has tried a new catheter-removal reminder for two months. The team now compares its CAUTI rate with the rate from before the reminder. Which PDSA step is this?

  1. Plan
  2. Do
  3. Study
  4. Act
Answer and rationale

C. Study. Comparing results with the baseline is the Study step.

A: Plan comes before the change, when the team names the problem and predicts the result.

B: Do is the two months of trying the reminder, which are already finished.

D: Act comes after Study, when the team decides to adopt, adapt, or abandon the reminder.
