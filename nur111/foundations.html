Clinical Decision Making: Learning Outcomes

Upon completion of this module, the student will be able to:

  1. Define clinical decision making and explain its importance in providing safe, effective care across the lifespan. (Knowledge)
  2. Identify the steps of the nursing process and their application in diverse age groups and health care settings. (Comprehension)
  3. Describe the components of the Clinical Judgment Model (Recognize Cues, Analyze Cues, Prioritize Hypotheses, Generate Solutions, Take Action, Evaluate Outcomes). (Comprehension)
  4. Apply the nursing process to assess, plan, implement, and evaluate care for individuals at different developmental stages. (Application)
  5. Use the Clinical Judgment Model to make appropriate decisions in clinical or simulated patient scenarios. (Application)
  6. Analyze patient data to differentiate between actual and potential problems across the lifespan. (Analysis)
  7. Develop age-appropriate nursing care plans based on clinical reasoning and patient priorities. (Synthesis)
  8. Reflect on personal decision-making processes and identify strategies to improve clinical judgment. (Evaluation)

Managing Care: Learning Outcomes

Upon completion of this module, the student will be able to:

  1. Define care management and its importance in providing safe, quality, and cost-effective nursing care across the lifespan. (Knowledge)
  2. Describe the role of the nurse in promoting cost-effective care while maintaining patient safety and quality outcomes. (Comprehension)
  3. Identify basic strategies for reducing waste and improving efficiency in the delivery of nursing care. (Comprehension)
  4. Explain how the intradisciplinary team contributes to effective care coordination and improved patient outcomes. (Comprehension)

  7. Apply the nursing process to prioritize patient care needs and delegate appropriately within the scope of practice. (Application)

Four thinking terms

Most errors new nurses make are thinking errors, not skill errors. In NCSBN's research on entry-level practice, a large share of errors by new nurses traced back to poor clinical decision making, and few employers were satisfied with new graduates' decision-making skills. That is why NCLEX now measures clinical judgment directly.

TermWhat it meansTime frame
Critical thinkingPurposeful, logical thinking. You question assumptions and consider alternatives.Any situation, clinical or not
Clinical reasoningWorking through one patient's data: sorting what is relevant and deciding what it means.One patient, one situation
Clinical judgmentThe decisions that come out of reasoning, shaped by experience, the patient's context, and the team. It builds over time.Across a shift, a stay, a career
Clinical decision makingChoosing an action from the options you generated. It is the point where judgment becomes something you do.The moment of choice

Clinical pearl

Judgment comes from practice, not from reading. Every case study, clinical day, and reflection adds to it. Right now you will write your thinking down step by step. With experience, the same steps will run in seconds.

The nursing process

The nursing process is the framework you already document in. It has five steps.

StepWhat you doThe question you ask
AssessmentCollect, organize, validate, and document subjective and objective data.What is going on with this patient?
AnalysisCompare findings to expected ranges, find patterns, and name the problems and risks.What does it mean, and what matters most?
PlanningSet measurable goals and choose evidence-based interventions.What outcome do I want, and how will I get there?
ImplementationCarry out the plan, document it, and supervise care you assign.What do I do now, and who does it?
EvaluationCheck the patient's response and revise the plan.Did it work? How do I know?

Key concept

Where it came from. Ida Jean Orlando studied nurse–patient interactions in the late 1950s and published her deliberative nursing process in 1961. Yura and Walsh described a four-step process in 1967, and nursing diagnosis was later added as a separate step, which gave us the five steps above.

RN and PN roles

RNPN
Uses all five steps.Works in four: data collection, planning, implementation, and evaluation, under RN supervision.
Does the initial and complex assessments, names the problems, and builds and changes the care plan.Collects data on stable patients, contributes to the plan, gives care within scope, and reports findings to the RN.

The Clinical Judgment Measurement Model

NCSBN built the Clinical Judgment Measurement Model (CJMM) to measure clinical judgment on NCLEX. ATI materials sometimes call it the "Clinical Judgment Action Model." It is the same model, and your outcomes and NCLEX use the name CJMM. The model has five layers, numbered 0 to 4.

Layer 3: the six functions

CJMM functionWhat you doThe questionNursing process
Recognize cuesPick out the data that matter from the history, the exam, labs, and the room.What matters most right now?Assessment
Analyze cuesLink cues to each other and to the patient's condition.What could these cues mean together?Analysis
Prioritize hypothesesRank the possible problems by urgency and risk.Which problem is most urgent, and why?Analysis
Generate solutionsSet the outcome you want and choose the actions that get there.What should happen, and what will I do?Planning
Take actionCarry out the highest-priority actions, including reporting.What do I do first?Implementation
Evaluate outcomesCompare what happened with what you expected.Did it work? What now?Evaluation

Key concept

It is a loop, not a line. When an outcome is not met, you go back to recognizing cues with the new information. Layer 2 runs underneath the whole time: you form hypotheses from the cues, refine them as you prioritize and plan, and evaluate them against what happens.

Clinical pearl

A cue is not a hypothesis. "SpO₂ dropped from 96% to 92%" is a cue: you can see it. "She has pneumonia" is a hypothesis: you are guessing what the cues mean. Students often skip straight to a hypothesis and then only look for cues that support it. Name the cues first.

Worked example

Read the vignette, then follow one pass through the six functions. Everything here comes from a basic assessment you already know how to do.

Patient vignette

"My bottom is sore. Can you hand me the TV remote?"
Mr. Ellis, 74, fictional composite. He is weak after an illness and has been in bed most of the past two days. At 0800 you help him with his bath. He has been lying on his back since 0200.
SacrumRed
Press testStays red
BriefDamp
PositionBack since 0200
  1. Recognize cuesA red area over the sacrum that stays red when you press it, a sore bottom, a damp brief, and six hours on his back.
  2. Analyze cuesPressure from lying in one position, plus moisture, plus redness that does not blanch. Together these point to early pressure damage. Redness on intact skin that does not blanch is how a stage 1 pressure injury is defined.
  3. Prioritize hypothesesThe skin comes before the TV remote. Pressure damage can get worse within hours, and it is preventable. Hand him the remote after you turn him.
  4. Generate solutionsGoal: no new or larger red areas at the next skin check. Actions: turn him off his sacrum, clean and dry the skin, change the brief, start a turning schedule, and report to the RN or instructor.
  5. Take actionTurn him to his side with pillows, clean and dry the area, change the brief, and tell the RN now.
  6. Evaluate outcomesCheck the area again at the next turn. If it is still red or larger, report again; the RN may add a pressure-relieving surface. Then loop back: why did this happen? Six hours without turning is the cue for the plan.

Safety flag

Redness that does not blanch when pressed is a must-report finding. Tell the RN or instructor during the shift, not at the end of the day, and do not massage the red area.

What shapes your judgment

Layer 4 of the model names the factors that make the same nurse decide differently on different days.

Environmental factorsIndividual factors
The setting and its safety hazardsYour knowledge base
Staffing levels and available resourcesYour level of experience and skill
The medical record and what it containsCognitive load: how much you are holding in your head
Task complexity and time pressureStress and fatigue
Consequences and risks of the decisionCultural considerations and your own assumptions

The patient adds a third layer of difficulty. Several conditions, many medications, and overlapping signs make cues harder to read. A side effect of one drug can look like dehydration, for example.

Clinical pearl

Fatigue raises the risk of error. When you notice you are rushing or tired, slow down on the steps that carry the most risk: medication checks, the cues that changed, and the call you are unsure about making.

Managing care

Care management is coordinating people, time, and supplies so each patient gets safe, high-quality care without waste. It is clinical judgment applied to resources.

Key concept

Cost-effective care does not mean cheaper care. It means getting the best outcome for the resources used. Skipping a needed turn to save time is not cost-effective. A pressure injury costs far more than the turn would have.

Ways nurses reduce waste

  • Check before you duplicate. Look for a lab already drawn or a test already done before collecting another.
  • Open only what you need. An opened, unused supply kit is thrown away.
  • Cluster care. Coordinate visits so the patient isn't woken or moved repeatedly and staff time isn't spent waiting.
  • Prevent complications. Turning, mobility, lung expansion, and fall precautions prevent the most expensive outcomes: extra hospital days.
  • Communicate changes early. A discharge, transport, or consult that is no longer needed should be canceled.

Clinical pearl

The most expensive waste on any unit is a preventable complication. Prevention is the cost-effective intervention.

The care team

Each team member brings something the others can't. Knowing who does what keeps care coordinated and keeps tasks with the person allowed to do them.

Team memberWhat they contribute
Registered nurse (RN)Assessment, care planning, initial teaching, evaluation, and coordinating the team. Accountable for the care assigned to others.
Licensed practical nurse (LPN)Care for stable patients with predictable outcomes, including medications and data collection, within state scope. Reinforces teaching and reports to the RN.
Unlicensed assistive personnel (UAP)Routine tasks such as vital signs, hygiene, mobility help, and intake and output. Reports findings to the nurse.
Case managerDischarge planning, insurance, and community resources. Moves the patient safely to the next setting.
Charge nurseAssignments, bed flow, and help when a patient's condition changes.
PharmacistMedication review, dosing, interactions, and alternatives.
Physical therapistMobility, strength, and safe-movement plans.
Respiratory therapistOxygen, airway treatments, and breathing exercises.
ProviderMedical diagnosis, orders, and the medical plan.

Safety flag

Assessment, initial teaching, evaluation, and care of an unstable patient stay with the RN. A UAP can measure a vital sign, but the RN decides what it means. You will learn the full delegation process in later courses.

Check yourself

Answer each question in your head, then open it to see the answer and why each choice is right or wrong. These are for practice only.

1. A nurse works through one patient's lab results and vital signs during a single shift and decides what they mean. What is this called?
  1. Critical thinking
  2. Clinical reasoning
  3. Clinical judgment
  4. Nursing diagnosis

Answer: B.

  • A. Critical thinking is broader. It applies to any situation, clinical or not.
  • B. Correct. Clinical reasoning is thinking through one patient's data in one situation.
  • C. Clinical judgment is wider. It is the decisions that come out of reasoning and build over time.
  • D. A nursing diagnosis is a product of analysis, not the thinking process itself.
2. Which of these is a cue, not a hypothesis?
  1. "She has pneumonia."
  2. "Her SpO₂ fell from 96% to 92% overnight."
  3. "She is at risk for falls."
  4. "I should call the provider."

Answer: B.

  • A. A guess about what cues mean. That is a hypothesis.
  • B. Correct. It is an observed piece of data. You can see it on the monitor.
  • C. A risk is a conclusion drawn from cues, so it is a hypothesis about a potential problem.
  • D. That is an action, from Take action.
3. The Analysis step of the nursing process holds which CJMM functions?
  1. Recognize cues only
  2. Analyze cues and Prioritize hypotheses
  3. Generate solutions and Take action
  4. Evaluate outcomes

Answer: B.

  • A. Recognize cues matches Assessment.
  • B. Correct. Analysis covers both making sense of cues and ranking the problems. New nurses most often skip the ranking.
  • C. These match Planning and Implementation.
  • D. This matches Evaluation.
4. A patient's goal was not met at the recheck. Where does clinical judgment go next?
  1. Document "goal not met" and continue the same plan
  2. Back to Recognize cues with the new data
  3. Straight to a new intervention
  4. Wait for the next scheduled assessment

Answer: B.

  • A. Repeating a plan that failed ignores the evaluation.
  • B. Correct. The model is a loop. New data may change what you think is happening.
  • C. Choosing a new action before rethinking the cues repeats the same thinking error.
  • D. A failed outcome is a reason to reassess now, not later.
5. Which action is cost-effective care?
  1. Opening a new supply kit each shift in case it is needed
  2. Checking whether a lab was already drawn before collecting another
  3. Skipping a scheduled turn to save time
  4. Asking for extra tests to be thorough

Answer: B.

  • A. Unused opened supplies are thrown away.
  • B. Correct. It avoids duplicate cost, blood loss, and discomfort.
  • C. A missed turn risks a pressure injury, which costs far more.
  • D. Tests without a reason add cost and burden without better outcomes.
6. A patient who lives alone can no longer go home today as planned. Which team member reworks the discharge plan?
  1. UAP
  2. Pharmacist
  3. Case manager
  4. Respiratory therapist

Answer: C.

  • A. Discharge planning is outside UAP scope.
  • B. The pharmacist reviews medications, not discharge arrangements.
  • C. Correct. The case manager coordinates discharge and resources. The RN keeps the case manager informed.
  • D. The respiratory therapist manages breathing treatments and oxygen.
