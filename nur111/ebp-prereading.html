What evidence-based practice is

Supports LO1LO2LO7

Many nursing routines were learned from whoever trained us. Some are backed by strong evidence, some are required by regulation, and some are just habit. EBP asks one question: Is our practice the best it can be, for this client?

Key concept

Evidence-based practice combines three inputs to make a care decision:

  • Best current evidence: well-designed research and the clinical guidelines built from it
  • Clinical expertise: the nurse’s assessment, skill, and judgment about this client
  • Client values and preferences: what matters to this person, including beliefs, culture, and goals

EBP applies at every stage of life. Placing infants on their backs to sleep came from evidence linking stomach sleeping to sudden infant death. Fall-prevention measures for older adults come from studies of why older adults fall in the hospital. In both cases, evidence changed what nurses do at the bedside and improved outcomes.

Evidence alone is not EBP. A guideline applied without judgment, or without asking the client, is just following a protocol. When a well-informed client declines an intervention and the nurse honors that choice, the nurse is still practicing EBP.

Not all evidence carries equal weight

Supports LO2LO7

For questions about what works, some kinds of evidence are less likely to be biased than others. From strongest to weakest:

Kind of evidenceWhat it is
Systematic review or meta-analysisPools the results of many studies on one question
Randomized controlled trialRandomly assigns people to an intervention or a comparison group
Cohort or case-control studyFollows or compares groups without random assignment
Descriptive, qualitative, or single studyDescribes a situation or people’s experiences
Expert opinion and traditionWhat experienced people believe or have always done

Clinical pearl

At the bedside you will rarely read a meta-analysis. You will use clinical practice guidelines and facility protocols, which experts build from the strongest evidence. When a routine has no evidence behind it, it is fair to ask why it is done.

For questions about what an experience is like, such as how clients feel about being woken at night, qualitative research is the right kind of evidence.

Asking a question you can search: PICOT

Supports LO7

Nurses turn practice problems into a focused question so they can search the literature. PICOT names the parts:

LetterStands forExample
PPopulationHospitalized adults 65 and older
IInterventionHand hygiene reminders at each doorway
CComparisonNo reminders
OOutcomeStaff hand hygiene compliance
TTimeOver 3 months

Put together: “On units caring for hospitalized adults 65 and older, do hand hygiene reminders at each doorway, compared with no reminders, improve staff hand hygiene compliance over 3 months?”

When best practice becomes the standard

Supports LO3LO5

Some best practices are strong and important enough to become required. Since January 1, 2026, The Joint Commission’s 14 National Performance Goals have replaced the National Patient Safety Goals for hospitals. Several shape what you will do on your first clinical day.

Safety flag

Identify every client with two identifiers, such as name and date of birth. Never use the room number. Label specimens in front of the client.

  • Infection prevention: hand hygiene that follows CDC or WHO guidelines
  • Medication management: reconcile the client’s medications, and give the client a written list at discharge
  • Workplace and patient safety: fall-prevention measures tailored to each client’s assessed risk
  • Pain management: offer nondrug treatments as well as medications

Best practice is applied to a person

Supports LO4LO6LO8

“Everyone kept telling me what the plan was. Nobody asked me what I was worried about.”
A composite of comments clients commonly make, not a real client

Care centered on the client’s preferences is linked to better satisfaction and outcomes. Nurses honor preferences in three ways:

  • Invite participation: bring the client into decisions, and keep advocating as needs change.
  • Promote understanding: explain in plain language and correct misunderstandings.
  • Share information: keep it a two-way conversation about the plan.

Watch for barriers you can control: medical jargon, routines set for staff convenience (a 0600 bath for someone who always bathes at night), and family wishes that override what the client wants. A competent adult client makes their own decisions.

Culture shapes beliefs about illness, diet, privacy, and who makes decisions. Start by learning the client’s own beliefs and practices, and examine your own assumptions. Don’t assume from a group label.

Clinical pearl

Ask early: “What matters most to you about your care today?” When a client declines a best practice, explore the reason first, then teach, then involve the provider and document.

Case: Mrs. Patel’s requests

Supports LO4LO6LO7

Mrs. Patel, 72, is on day 2 of an admission for cellulitis of the left leg. She walks with a walker. Her fall-risk screening places her at high risk, and the unit’s fall protocol includes a bed alarm.

Age72
Admitted forCellulitis, L leg
MobilityWalker
Fall riskHigh

Mrs. Patel tells you, “I’ve always bathed at night, not first thing in the morning. And please turn off that bed alarm. It goes off every time I roll over, and it’s embarrassing.”

1. Which request can you simply honor, and why?

The bath at night. Bath timing is a routine, not a safety standard. Moving it respects her preference at no cost to her safety.

2. How do you respond to the bed alarm request?

Don’t just say yes or no. Ask what bothers her most about it, then explain why the team is concerned about falls. Then look at other ways to keep her safe that fit her wishes: call light and walker within reach, bed in the low position, scheduled help to the bathroom, nonslip footwear. Talk with the charge nurse about whether the protocol allows a change, and document the conversation. The decision combines all three EBP inputs: the evidence, your assessment of her, and her preference.

3. Write a PICOT question this situation raises.

One possibility: “In hospitalized older adults at high risk for falls (P), do bed alarms (I), compared with scheduled toileting rounds without alarms (C), reduce falls (O) during hospitalization (T)?” Any question that names a population, an intervention, a comparison, an outcome, and a time frame works.

Check yourself

Supports LO1LO2LO7

These are for practice only and are not graded. Commit to an answer before you open the explanation.

Supports LO1LO2

1. Which statement best describes evidence-based practice?

  1. Doing what the most recent study recommends
  2. Following the unit’s routine because it has always worked
  3. Combining research evidence, clinical expertise, and the client’s preferences
  4. Doing whatever the client prefers
Answer and rationale

C. EBP integrates all three inputs.

A: One study is weak evidence on its own, and research alone leaves out judgment and the client.

B: Tradition is not evidence. “We’ve always done it this way” is the habit EBP is meant to question.

D: Preferences are one input, not the only one. The nurse also brings evidence and judgment.

Supports LO2

2. A nurse wants to know whether a new oral care routine prevents pneumonia. Which source gives the strongest evidence?

  1. An experienced nurse’s recommendation
  2. A systematic review of several randomized trials
  3. One hospital’s report of its own results
  4. Interviews with clients about their oral care
Answer and rationale

B. A systematic review pools the results of many well-designed trials, so it is the least likely to be biased for a question about what works.

A: Expert opinion is the weakest level of evidence.

C: One site’s results are a single study and may not apply elsewhere.

D: Interviews tell you about experiences, not whether the routine prevents pneumonia.

Supports LO7

3. In the question “In adults after abdominal surgery, does early walking, compared with bed rest, reduce the length of hospital stay?”, what is the C?

  1. Adults after abdominal surgery
  2. Early walking
  3. Bed rest
  4. Length of hospital stay
Answer and rationale

C. Bed rest is the comparison.

A: This is the Population.

B: This is the Intervention.

D: This is the Outcome. (This question has no T. A complete PICOT question would add a time frame.)
